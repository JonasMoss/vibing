# The demo problem — "find me these papers"

*Day 2, 10:30. This is the worked example the instructor drives; you do the same
thing afterwards with a topic of your own.*

## The situation

You are writing the opening of a paper — or a teaching note, or a research-methods
seminar — about **the argument over statistical significance**: the `p < 0.05`
threshold, the calls to abandon it, and what people propose instead.

You half-remember the literature. That is the normal state of affairs.

## What you actually have

> - There's an official statement from the **American Statistical Association** about
>   p-values. 2016, I think?
> - A **Nature** piece about retiring statistical significance — signed by hundreds
>   of people.
> - Something called **"the garden of forking paths."** Gelman?
> - A **psychology** paper showing you can prove anything if you're flexible enough
>   with the analysis.
> - Someone proposed just **lowering the threshold to 0.005**. There was a rebuttal
>   saying justify it instead.
> - And a journal that **banned p-values outright.**

## What to ask for

> For each of these, find the actual paper. Give me the full citation — all authors,
> exact title, journal, volume, issue, pages, year — and the **DOI**. Download the
> ones that are freely available into `references/pdf/`. For anything you cannot get,
> list the DOI in `references/to-fetch.md` so I can request it from the library.
>
> **Tell me which ones you are not sure about.** If you cannot find something, say so
> — do not guess.

## Why the DOI is the whole game

A title can look right. Authors can look right. A journal can look right. A **DOI
either resolves to that exact paper, or it does not.** It is the one field you can
check in a single click — paste it after `https://doi.org/` and see what comes back.

It is also what you hand your librarian when the PDF is behind a paywall.

And it is the tripwire: **a fabricated citation usually has a fabricated or missing
DOI.** Ask for the DOI every time, and hallucination has almost nowhere left to hide.

## Then do it for your own work

Same shape, your topic:

1. Write down what you half-remember — vague is fine, that's the point.
2. Ask the agent to resolve each one, with DOIs.
3. Check three of them yourself. Actually paste the DOI into your browser.
4. Note which it got right, which it fumbled, and which it admitted to not knowing.

That last column is the one that tells you how much to trust it next time.
