# examples/syllabus — instructor key for the course factory (Day 3, 10:30–12:00)

Handout: `handouts/course-factory.md`. Prop:
`ORG3402-organisational-behaviour.md` — a fictional BI bachelor course in
organisational behaviour, 7.5 ECTS, twelve sessions, full assessment structure
and reading list. Synthetic and safe to hand out. Every citation in the reading
list is real and was checked before it went in; the *course* is invented, the
*literature* is not.

**The prop is the fallback, not the starting point.** Participants lead with their
own course, described from memory in five lines. Offering the syllabus first gives
the whole room permission to disengage from their own material, and the entire
point of the block is that they find something wrong with *their* outcomes, not
with Ingrid Halvorsen's. Hand it to the three or four people who freeze, and to
anyone who'd rather not expose their own syllabus to the neighbour.

---

## What is planted, and what happens when the agent hits it

### The vague outcomes

Three of the eight outcomes are mush. They are not labelled anywhere in the
syllabus, and they are written the way real ones are written — which is why
nobody notices them until a rubric is built on top.

| | Outcome | Why it collapses |
|---|---|---|
| **K2** | "understands the importance of organisational culture for organisational performance" | No performance, no object, no observable. "Understands" and "importance" both refuse to name anything a marker could see. |
| **S3** | "is able to reflect critically on leadership in contemporary organisations" | "Reflect critically" is a mood, not a behaviour. Nothing distinguishes an A from a C except the marker's taste. |
| **G2** | "has developed an awareness of the ethical dimensions of managerial decisions and of the value of diversity in working life" | Borderline, and deliberately so: this is exactly how general-competence outcomes get written in practice. Nobody has ever assessed "an awareness". |

The other five (K1, K3, S1, S2, G1) are sharp on purpose. **K1 vs K2 and S1 vs S3
are the contrast that does the teaching** — same document, same author, same
afternoon, wildly different assessability.

**What the agent actually does with them.** It does not refuse and it does not
complain. It writes a rubric. For K2 you will reliably get four levels along the
lines of *"demonstrates excellent understanding of the importance of culture /
good understanding / some understanding / limited understanding"* — the same
sentence in four costumes, with no observable in any of them. For S1 you get rows
like *"names a theory, correctly identifies the relevant construct, and derives
two actions each traceable to a named element"* — which a marker can apply at
speed with a class of 200.

That side-by-side is the payload of the caveat section in the handout. **Get it on
the projector.** One participant's K2 row next to their S1 row is worth more than
any explanation.

**The line to say out loud:** the rubric came out exactly as sharp as the outcome
went in. That is a fact about the syllabus, not about the model.

### The structural traps (bonus, if anyone asks the right question)

Nobody is told these exist. They surface in seconds if someone asks the agent to
map the document against itself — see the "if you're ahead" move below.

1. **Two of the twelve sessions are assessed by nothing.** Sessions 6–7 (teams)
   and session 12 (organisational change) have no learning outcome that covers
   them. Leadership (sessions 9–10) is covered only by S3, the mush one. So a
   third of the course is assessed either not at all or vaguely.
2. **The vague outcomes have no real assessment home.** Part 1 names the outcomes
   it assesses (K1, K3, S1, S2, G1). Part 2 says "assesses all learning
   outcomes" — which is where K2, S3 and G2 quietly live, and nowhere else.
   The catch-all is doing all the work.
3. **A promise with nothing behind it.** The course description says students
   "practise giving and receiving structured peer feedback", and the teaching
   activities repeat it. Peer feedback appears in no session and no assessment
   component. This is the same trap family as the Day-1 handbook, so if the room
   did that block, the reflex is already there.
4. **A soft contradiction:** "Attendance is not assessed. The reading, however, is
   not optional." Minor, and a good test of whether the agent flags real
   inconsistencies or manufactures them.

## Good and bad generated questions

Participants will not know what to make of their twelve drafts. Have these ready.

**Bad, and why — all three come out of this syllabus unprompted:**

- *"Discuss the importance of organisational culture for organisational
  performance."* — This is K2 with a question mark on it. Answerable from the
  stem alone, no correct answer, two examiners will not agree, and a model that
  has never attended will produce 600 competent words.
- *"Critically reflect on leadership in contemporary organisations."* — S3,
  likewise. The tell: you cannot state the full-marks answer in one sentence.
- *"What are Herzberg's hygiene factors?"* — Not vague, but pure recall of a list.
  Fine as one mark in a short-answer section, fatal as an essay question.

**Good, and why:**

- *"A logistics firm raised salaries by 12% and refurbished the office. Six months
  later, engagement scores are unchanged and turnover is up. Using Herzberg's
  two-factor theory, explain this result, then state two specific actions the
  manager should take instead — naming, for each, the element of the theory it
  follows from."* — Hits S1 exactly. Unseen situation, a defensible correct
  answer, and the "name the element" clause is what makes it markable at speed.
- *"Jehn (1995) and De Dreu & Weingart (2003) reach different conclusions about
  task conflict. State the difference, give one plausible reason for it, and say
  which you would rely on when advising a project team. Justify your choice."* —
  K3. Forces a commitment, which is what makes it discriminate.
- *"Two team descriptions follow. Classify the conflict in each as primarily task
  or primarily relationship conflict, quoting the evidence in the text that
  decided it for you."* — S2. Observable, fast to mark, and the quoting
  requirement kills bluffing.

**The pattern to name in plenary:** every good question above names a performance
and supplies a situation. Every bad one names a topic. That is the same
distinction as sharp-vs-mush outcomes, one level down — which is the point.

## The subtly-wrong answers this material invites

The Herzberg one is the flagship. Know the actual theory well enough to
adjudicate live, because someone will argue.

### Herzberg's two-factor theory — what it actually says

Frederick Herzberg's two-factor (motivation–hygiene) theory, from *The Motivation
to Work* (1959) and the much-reprinted "One more time: How do you motivate
employees?" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1968, 46(1), 53–62):

- **Hygiene factors** are extrinsic — they belong to the *context* of the job:
  company policy and administration, supervision, relationships with supervisor
  and peers, working conditions, **salary**, status, security. When they are
  inadequate, people are dissatisfied. When you fix them, dissatisfaction goes
  away — and *nothing else happens*.
- **Motivators** are intrinsic — they belong to the *content* of the job:
  achievement, recognition for achievement, the work itself, responsibility,
  advancement, growth. These are what produce satisfaction and effort.
- **The structural claim, which is the whole theory:** satisfaction and
  dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of a single scale. The opposite of job
  satisfaction is *no* job satisfaction, not dissatisfaction. Two separate
  continua, driven by two separate sets of factors.
- **The prescription** is therefore job enrichment — vertical loading, adding
  responsibility and control — not job enlargement, which just adds more tasks of
  the same kind.

**The classic conflation, and the one this syllabus is built to produce:** treating
hygiene factors as motivators. In its crude form the answer lists "competitive
salary" and "good working conditions" among the motivators. That version is too
easy to spot.

**The version to ask for** is the one that gets the taxonomy *right* in paragraph
one and then collapses it in paragraph three: correctly lists both sets, then
recommends that the manager "raise salaries and improve the office in order to
increase motivation." Structurally fluent, correctly cited, and it has quietly
abandoned the two-continua claim that the whole theory exists to make. That is
what a tired marker at script 40 waves through — and it is precisely the shape of
AI-written student work.

**Useful thing to have in your pocket** if the discussion turns into "but is
Herzberg even right": the standard objection is that the result is an artefact of
the critical-incident method — people credit good episodes to themselves and blame
bad ones on their surroundings. House & Wigdor (1967), *Personnel Psychology*,
20(4), 369–390, is the classic review making that case, and it's in the syllabus
under recommended reading.

### Others this syllabus sets up

Each is a *substantive error inside a fluent paragraph*, which is what the Rung 3
prompt asks for. If a group's generated answer is wrong in a boring way, hand them
one of these.

- **Contingent reward filed as transformational.** In Bass's full-range model,
  transformational leadership comprises idealised influence, inspirational
  motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualised consideration.
  *Contingent reward* and management-by-exception are the **transactional** side.
  The subtler and better error: claiming transactional leadership is simply
  ineffective. Judge & Piccolo (2004) put transformational at .44 and contingent
  reward at .39 — close enough that "transactional doesn't work" is wrong, and
  laissez-faire at −.37, i.e. actively bad.
- **"Task conflict is good for teams."** Jehn (1995) found conditions under which
  task conflict helps. De Dreu & Weingart's (2003) meta-analysis found strong
  *negative* correlations for both relationship **and** task conflict with team
  performance and satisfaction — explicitly contrary to the prediction and to the
  textbook claim — with the negative relations stronger on complex tasks. An
  answer that cites the meta-analysis *as support for* the benefit of task
  conflict is a beautiful subtle error, because the citation is real and the
  reading of it is not.
- **"Extrinsic rewards destroy intrinsic motivation."** Too strong. Deci, Koestner
  & Ryan (1999) found that expected, tangible rewards contingent on engagement,
  completion or performance undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation — while
  positive feedback and verbal praise *enhanced* it (d ≈ .33 and .31). The
  overreach is the error, and it's the one most likely to appear in real student
  work.
- **Tuckman as a longitudinal study.** Tuckman (1965) was a synthesis of existing
  literature, not a longitudinal test of teams; "adjourning" was added later
  (Tuckman & Jensen, 1977). "Tuckman's longitudinal study of work groups
  identified five stages" is confidently, citably wrong.
- **Schein's levels inverted.** Deepest to surface: basic underlying assumptions
  (taken for granted, largely unconscious), espoused beliefs and values,
  artifacts (visible, easy to observe, hard to decipher). The error is calling
  espoused values the deepest level, or treating the artifacts as "the real
  culture".
- **Lewin's steps attributed to Kotter.** Unfreeze–change–refreeze is Lewin.
  Kotter's eight steps begin with establishing a sense of urgency, not with
  creating a vision — a step-order error is very hard to spot at speed.

## Timing — the 90-minute block

| Clock | What | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:08 | Frame it, then demo Rung 0 live with **your own** course, out loud, speech-to-text, in 60 seconds | The demo is the permission slip. Nobody prepared; show them nothing was needed. |
| 0:08–0:20 | Rung 0 — everyone gets a `course.md` | Walk the room. Hand the ORG 3402 prop to whoever has stalled. Don't announce it to everyone. |
| 0:20–0:35 | Rung 1 — twelve questions, plus the one-sentence full-marks answers | The one-sentence clause is what makes this rung worth 15 minutes. |
| 0:35–0:50 | Rung 2 — the rubric | **Interrupt at ~0:48:** "Hands up — whose rubric has a row that says *excellent understanding / good understanding / some understanding*?" Most hands go up. Say nothing more. Bank it. |
| 0:50–1:05 | Rung 3 — the three answers | **The peak of the block. Protect this fifteen minutes at the cost of anything else.** |
| 1:05–1:12 | Integrity beat, plenary | Ask: "did anyone's subtly-wrong answer outscore the mediocre one?" Somebody's did. That's the discussion. |
| 1:12–1:22 | Rungs 4 and 5 — mark, then critique the questions | Move fast, this is momentum not depth. |
| 1:22–1:30 | Land the caveat, with the callback to 0:48 | Everyone leaves having rewritten **one** learning outcome. That's the exit ticket. |

## If the room is 20 minutes ahead

Don't add rungs — deepen. In rough order of value:

1. **Swap and mark.** Pairs exchange one question and one rubric, and each marks
   the *other's* subtly-wrong answer using the *other's* rubric. Fifteen minutes,
   and it exposes rubric ambiguity more brutally than any critique prompt. This is
   the best ahead-move by a distance.
2. **The coverage map** — a 30-second demo with real impact, and it works on their
   own syllabus as well as the prop:

   > Make a table: every session on one axis, every learning outcome on the
   > other. Mark which sessions serve which outcomes. Then tell me which sessions
   > serve no outcome, and which outcomes are taught in no session.

   On ORG 3402 this surfaces traps 1 and 2 above in one shot. On a participant's
   own syllabus it produces a short silence, which is the good kind.
3. **Rung 6** from the handout: question variants for next year, the printable
   marking sheet, the Norwegian version.

## If the room is 20 minutes behind

Cut in this order, and no other:

1. **Rung 5 (the question critique) — cut entirely.** It's the most satisfying
   rung and the least necessary; it can be a single prompt read aloud from the
   front.
2. **Rung 4 — mark one answer, not three.** The subtly-wrong one. That's the only
   one that carries an argument.
3. **Rung 1 — six questions, not twelve.** Two per difficulty level. Keep the
   one-sentence full-marks answers; that clause is doing more work than the extra
   six questions.

**Never cut Rung 3 or the caveat.** If you are catastrophically behind, run Rung 3
as a plenary demo on the projector using ORG 3402 — four minutes, everyone reads
the subtly-wrong answer together — then go straight to the caveat with a K2 rubric
you generated beforehand. That version still lands the two things the block exists
for: what AI-written work looks like from the marking side, and that rubric
quality is capped by outcome quality.
