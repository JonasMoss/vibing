## 1. Herzberg — two-factor / motivation–hygiene (10 min)

### the study

Herzberg, Mausner & Snyderman 1959, *The Motivation to Work*.
~200 accountants and engineers (203 I think), nine sites around Pittsburgh.

Method = **critical incident technique**. Two questions, basically:
- think of a time you felt exceptionally GOOD about your job. tell me the story.
- now a time you felt exceptionally BAD.

(worth saying out loud that this is an *interview* study, 1959, one city, two
professions, all men presumably. sets up the critique later without me having to
argue against it)

### the finding

The good stories and the bad stories were about **different things**. Not more or
less of the same thing — different categories entirely.

| the good stories were about | the bad stories were about |
|---|---|
| achievement | company policy & administration |
| recognition | supervision (the boss, technically incompetent) |
| the work itself | relationship with supervisor |
| responsibility | working conditions |
| advancement | salary |
| growth | interpersonal relations / peers |
| | status |
| | job security |
| | personal life |

→ he calls the left column **motivators**, the right column **hygiene factors**
(as in sanitation — hygiene doesn't make you healthy, it stops you getting sick)

note the pattern: motivators are all *intrinsic to the work being done*, hygiene
is all *the context surrounding the work*. that's the actual structure. say it.

### the claim that people get wrong

This is THE point of Herzberg and every year half the exam answers miss it:

satisfaction and dissatisfaction are **not two ends of one scale**. they are two
separate scales.

- opposite of job satisfaction = NO job satisfaction (not dissatisfaction)
- opposite of dissatisfaction = NO dissatisfaction (not satisfaction)

practical consequence, and this is the sellable line:
> fixing hygiene stops people being unhappy. it does not make them motivated.
> you can have a job with a fine salary, a decent boss and clean offices that
> nobody cares about at all.

good in-class example: the office move. new building, better chairs, nicer
canteen. two weeks of goodwill and then baseline. nobody's work changed.

### KITA

Herzberg 1968 HBR, "One More Time: How Do You Motivate Employees?" — apparently
the most reprinted HBR article ever (TODO: check that, I've said it for years and
never verified it)

KITA = kick in the ass. three flavours:
- negative physical (illegal now, mostly)
- negative psychological (the cruelty is deniable, so it survives)
- positive KITA — rewards, bonuses, perks

his argument: all three produce **movement**, not **motivation**. the difference:
movement needs a new kick every time. motivation runs on its own battery.
> "if I kick my dog, he moves. when I want him to move again, what must I do?
> kick him again." (paraphrase — find the exact line, it lands better verbatim)

positive KITA is the interesting one for this audience — it looks like
motivation, it's popular with everybody, and it's still a kick.

### what he told managers to do

**job enrichment** (vertical loading): give people more responsibility, more
autonomy, direct feedback, a whole task rather than a fragment, new and harder
things over time.

NOT **job enlargement** (horizontal): giving someone three boring tasks instead
of one boring task. "adding zero to zero" — I think that's his phrase.

(the enrichment/enlargement distinction is a reliable exam question. flag it.)

### the critique — do NOT skip this

Herzberg is in every textbook and it is the *least* well-supported of the three.
Students should know both facts at once.

- **method-bound.** the two clean columns show up when you use the critical
  incident interview. use another method — rating scales, whatever — and the
  clusters do not separate like that. a theory that only appears through one
  instrument is a finding about the instrument.
- **attribution bias / self-serving bias.** ask someone why they felt great and
  they tell you about themselves (I achieved, I was recognised). ask why they
  felt awful and they tell you about the environment (policy, my boss, the
  building). Herzberg's two columns are *exactly* what that bias would produce.
  Vroom made this argument early (1964?) — check the cite.
- salary shows up in **both** columns in his own data. he calls it hygiene
  anyway. hold that thought for part 4.
- he never measured whether satisfied people actually *perform* better.

so why still teach it? because the *distinction* survived even though the
evidence for the two-factor structure didn't: context factors and content
factors do different work. that idea goes straight into Hackman & Oldham's job
characteristics model (skill variety, task identity, task significance,
autonomy, feedback → 1976) which is much better supported. mention JCM in one
sentence, they meet it in ORG 3405.

## 2. Self-determination theory — Deci & Ryan (12 min)

careful with time here. this is the part I always overrun.

SDT is not one theory, it's a family (six mini-theories at last count). they need
two pieces of it: the needs, and the continuum.

### three basic psychological needs

universal, not preferences. thwart them and you get worse motivation AND worse
wellbeing.

- **autonomy** — my behaviour is mine, I stand behind it
- **competence** — I can do this, I'm getting better
- **relatedness** — I matter to people here, and they to me

**autonomy is NOT independence.** biggest misreading, correct it explicitly.
you can autonomously do what your boss asked, if you see the point of it. and you
can be entirely independent and feel completely coerced. autonomy = volition, not
solitude. (Norwegian students hear "selvstendighet" and go straight to
independence — say this in Norwegian too)

### intrinsic vs extrinsic — and why that's the wrong cut

start with the naive version they already have:
- intrinsic = I do it because it's interesting
- extrinsic = I do it for something else

then break it. "extrinsic" is doing enormous work in that sentence. compare:
- I file the report because my boss will yell at me
- I file the report because sloppy records genuinely hurt patients and I care

both extrinsic. nothing else about them is alike.

### the internalization continuum (the good slide)

amotivation → external → introjected → identified → integrated → intrinsic

- **amotivation** — no intent, why bother
- **external regulation** — reward, punishment, deadline, someone watching
- **introjected** — the boss is now *inside your head*. guilt, shame, anxiety,
  "I'd feel like a fraud if I didn't". self-esteem held hostage. (this is the one
  students recognise instantly and have never had a word for. spend a minute.)
- **identified** — I personally value this outcome, even if the task is dull
- **integrated** — it fits who I am, coherent with my other values
- **intrinsic** — the activity itself is the reward

The useful cut is NOT intrinsic vs extrinsic. It's:
- **autonomous motivation** = identified + integrated + intrinsic
- **controlled motivation** = external + introjected

and the managerial punchline, which is realistic rather than utopian:
> most work will never be intrinsically fun. nobody is going to love reconciling
> expense claims. the target is **identified** regulation — people who see why it
> matters. that is achievable, and it behaves almost like intrinsic motivation.

### the undermining effect

Deci 1971 — Soma puzzle experiments. Students solve puzzles; half get paid per
puzzle solved. Then the experimenter leaves the room ("I need to fetch something")
and the real measure is what they do in the free-choice period with magazines
available. The **paid** group played with the puzzles less.

pay them for something they already enjoyed → they enjoy it less.

Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, *Psychological Bulletin*, 128 experiments. roughly:
- tangible rewards that are **expected** and **contingent** on doing / completing
  / performing → undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation
- **unexpected** rewards → don't undermine
- **verbal** rewards / positive feedback → *enhance* intrinsic motivation

why: a reward can be read two ways. as **control** ("I'm doing this for the
money" — locus of causality shifts outward, autonomy down) or as **information**
("I'm good at this" — competence up). same cash, different meaning, opposite
effect. the meaning is set by how it's delivered, not by the amount.

honest note: Cameron & Pierce ran a competing meta-analysis (1994) and concluded
the undermining effect is small and limited. genuinely contested. I'll say the
lab effect is real and the field question is part 4.

### what an autonomy-supportive manager actually does

not vague. four behaviours, all learnable:
1. give a **rationale** — why this task, why now
2. **acknowledge** that the boring parts are boring (do not pretend)
3. offer **choice** where choice actually exists — the how, the order, the
   deadline. even small ones.
4. drop **controlling language** — "you must", "you should", "I need you to"

vs a controlling manager: surveillance, deadlines used as threats, evaluative
pressure, contingent praise ("*good boy*" praise), competition between colleagues.

## 3. Goal-setting theory — Locke & Latham (10 min)

The best-evidenced theory in the whole course. Say that. Students assume the
famous one is the true one; here the famous one *is* the true one, and it's not
Maslow.

Locke & Latham 1990 (book), and the summary paper: **2002, *American
Psychologist*, "Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task
motivation: a 35-year odyssey"**. 400+ studies, tens of thousands of people,
many countries, lab and field.

### the core finding

**specific, difficult goals produce higher performance than easy goals, "do your
best" goals, or no goals.**

both words matter:
- **specific** — "cut response time to 24 hours", not "improve responsiveness".
  vague goals have no stopping rule, so any effort counts as compliance.
- **difficult** — and this is the counterintuitive half: performance rises
  *linearly* with goal difficulty, right up to the ceiling of ability. hard goals
  beat easy goals even though hard goals are met less often.

"do your best" is the worst instruction in management. it sounds encouraging and
it is functionally empty.

### why it works (mediators) — four

1. **direction** — attention goes to goal-relevant activity, away from the rest
2. **effort** — harder goal, more energy mobilised, proportionally
3. **persistence** — you keep going longer (though with a fixed deadline you may
   work faster instead)
4. **strategy** — hardest goals push people to *search for a better method*, not
   just push harder. this is the underrated one.

### when it doesn't work (moderators) — five

1. **commitment** — the goal must be accepted. highest when (a) it matters to
   the person and (b) they believe they can hit it (self-efficacy). assigned
   goals work fine *if* a rationale is given. (note the link back to SDT —
   rationale again)
2. **feedback** — goals without progress feedback do almost nothing. feedback
   without goals does almost nothing. they are a **pair**. underline this.
3. **task complexity** — the effect shrinks as tasks get complex. on complex work
   the bottleneck is knowing *how*, and a goal can't supply that.
4. **ability** — a goal cannot exceed the ceiling
5. **situational constraints** — no resources, no result

### learning goals vs performance goals

Important, and the practical part they can use.

Novel or complex task + specific difficult **performance** goal = often *worse*
performance. People go into frantic effort mode and stop searching for a method.
Tunnel vision under pressure.

Fix: set a **learning goal** instead. "find and test five ways to shorten the
handover" rather than "cut handover time 30%". Once the method is known, switch
to the performance goal. (Winters & Latham, Seijts & Latham — check who exactly)

### SMART — mention, then qualify

They've all met SMART goals. Worth 60 seconds: SMART is a folk simplification and
the **A/R** (achievable / realistic) actively contradicts the theory's finding
that *difficult* goals win. what the theory actually requires: specific,
difficult, committed to, and fed back on.

### goals gone wild

Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman 2009, *Academy of Management
Perspectives* — "Goals Gone Wild: the systematic side effects of overprescribing
goal setting". Argument: goal setting is a prescription drug, dispensed like
candy. Documented side effects:

- **narrow focus** — the unmeasured parts of the job degrade
- **unethical behaviour** — if the goal is the only thing that counts, and you're
  just short, people close the gap by whatever means
- **risk-taking** — a fixed target makes gambling rational when you're behind
- **crowds out intrinsic motivation** — the task becomes the number
- **corrodes culture** — especially with stretch goals + individual competition

examples:
- **Sears** auto centres, early 90s: mechanics on a sales quota per hour →
  unnecessary repairs sold to customers. quota withdrawn.
- **Ford Pinto**: "under 2000 pounds and under 2000 dollars" → design compromises
  and the fuel tank. (careful, this one gets told sloppily. keep it to: a vivid
  specific goal, safety wasn't in it.)
- **Wells Fargo**, 2016: cross-selling target of eight products per household →
  millions of accounts opened without customers knowing. best modern case, and
  the target itself was reportedly picked because "eight rhymes with great"
  (TODO: find that reference — I'm fairly sure it's in the Stumpf shareholder
  letters, verify before saying it out loud)

Locke & Latham replied in the same issue, roughly: side effects come from bad
goals and bad systems, not from goal setting. Both sides are worth 2 minutes; it
is the best live disagreement in the syllabus.

The synthesis I want them to write down:
> a goal is a very sharp instrument. it will cut exactly where you point it, and
> nowhere else. so be careful what you point it at.

## 4. Does pay motivate? (8 min — this is the part they came for)

Set it up as a genuine puzzle. Everyone "knows" money doesn't motivate (they've
seen the TED talk) and everyone also negotiates their salary. Both intuitions are
doing real work. Sort them out.

### what the evidence actually says

**pay level barely predicts satisfaction.**
Judge, Piccolo, Podsakoff, Shaw & Rich 2010, *Journal of Vocational Behavior*.
Meta-analysis, 115 correlations from 92 samples.
- pay level ↔ job satisfaction: **r ≈ .15**
- pay level ↔ pay satisfaction: **r ≈ .23**

that's ~2% of the variance in job satisfaction. doctors earning five times what
nurses earn are not five times more satisfied. barely more at all.

**incentives raise quantity, not quality.**
Jenkins, Mitra, Gupta & Shaw 1998, *Journal of Applied Psychology*. 39 studies.
- financial incentives ↔ performance **quantity**: r ≈ .34 (that's real, that's
  not nothing)
- financial incentives ↔ performance **quality**: not significant

one sentence version:
> pay for output and you get more output. you do not get better output.
> if quality is what you care about, you are paying for the wrong thing.

**sometimes money makes it worse.**
- Gneezy & Rustichini, "A Fine Is a Price" — Haifa day-care centres introduce a
  small fine for picking your child up late. late pickups **went up**. and when
  the fine was removed they stayed up. a moral obligation ("I'm inconveniencing a
  teacher") got converted into a market transaction ("I'm buying twenty extra
  minutes, cheap"), and you can't convert it back. this is the hook, use it early
  if I didn't use Wells Fargo.
- Gneezy & Rustichini, "Pay Enough or Don't Pay At All" (QJE 2000) — small
  payments produced *worse* performance than no payment. the relationship isn't
  monotonic. offering someone 20 kroner reframes a favour as a lousy job.
- Ariely, Gneezy, Loewenstein & Mazar 2009 — very large bonuses (up to several
  months' spending, field experiment in rural India) made performance **worse**
  on tasks needing memory, concentration and creativity. fine on the purely
  mechanical task. choking under pressure, with money as the pressure.

**the measurement problem.** whenever a job has a measurable part and an
unmeasurable part, paying for the measurable part pulls effort out of the rest.
teaching to the test, surgeons declining hard cases, call centres closing tickets
fast and badly. (economists: Holmström & Milgrom, multitasking. don't name it
unless someone asks.)

**fairness beats level.** Adams' equity theory (1963/65): people don't evaluate
their pay, they evaluate their *ratio* of outcomes to inputs against somebody
else's. so the salary that felt fine in September feels insulting in October
because of one thing you found out about a colleague. nothing about the money
changed.
- there's the UC salary-disclosure study — a website made colleagues' salaries
  public; people below the median got less satisfied, people above didn't get
  happier. asymmetric. (Card, Mas, Moretti & Saez? AER, around 2012 — TODO: find
  that reference properly, I have the finding right and the cite half-right)

### the counter-argument (include it, they get a one-sided story everywhere else)

Gerhart & Fang 2015, *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and OB* —
"revisiting long-held beliefs". Argues the crowding-out story is oversold outside
the lab; well-designed pay-for-performance does raise performance in real firms,
and part of the effect is **sorting** — who applies, who stays, who leaves. That
mechanism is invisible in a lab study with undergraduates and a puzzle.

Also, obviously: pay's biggest job may not be daily effort at all. It's
**attraction and retention**. Nobody is arguing you should pay people badly.

### the landing

Four lines on the last slide:

1. pay is a **necessary condition and a weak lever** — get it right, then stop
   expecting it to do work it can't do
2. **fairness beats level.** comparisons, not amounts
3. incentives work on **quantity of simple, measurable, individual output** and
   backfire on **judgement, quality, cooperation, creativity**
4. the things that actually move discretionary effort are the boring ones from
   parts 1–3: interesting work, autonomy, competence, colleagues who matter,
   clear hard goals with real feedback

and the callback to the opening question — "who has done their best work for
money?" — nobody put their hand up, and now they know why that isn't a
coincidence.
