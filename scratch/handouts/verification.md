# The routine

**Day 3, afternoon.** The one page worth keeping.

Everything on this sheet exists for one reason: **so you can move faster.** Not
slower. You can hand your real work to a machine precisely because you know how
to catch it when it goes wrong. Checking is not the brake. It is the thing that
lets you take the corner.

> **The whole course in one line:** be bold, because you can check.

---

## The five habits

You already did all five this week. Here they are with their names on.

### 1. Plan before execute

> Tell me what you're going to do. Don't change anything yet.

You used this on Wednesday, on the messy folder, before letting it touch a single
file. It costs one extra prompt and it is where you do your actual job — the
plan is the thing you have an opinion about.

**Argue with the plan.** If you approve every plan you are handed, you are not
supervising anything.

### 2. Save first, then be reckless

> Commit this before we start.

The snapshot is what converts "what if it ruins something" into "let it try."

One honest detail: git restores what was saved **before** things went wrong. A
snapshot taken after the mess is a snapshot of the mess. **Save point, then
courage** — in that order.

### 3. Make it show you the evidence

Not "are you sure?" — it will say yes. Ask for the thing you can check yourself:

> Give me the DOI for each of those.

> Quote the sentence you got that from.

> Which of these did you actually open, and which are you going from memory?

A claim you can check in ten seconds is worth more than a paragraph of
reassurance.

### 4. Spot-check exactly one row

You built a table from ten PDFs on Thursday. You did not re-read ten PDFs. You
picked **one cell** and went and looked.

That is the whole technique, and it works because errors are rarely lonely. If
the one row you picked is clean, you have real evidence about the other nine. If
it wobbles, you have found out cheaply.

**Pick the row you'd be most embarrassed to get wrong.**

### 5. Never paste what you haven't read

The one with your name on it at the end. Not "never use it" — *read it first.*

## Two questions that outperform any caution list

Most "be careful with AI" advice is unusable in the moment. These two are not:

> **What did you not check?**

> **That was shallow. Go deeper.**

The first surfaces the gap the confident summary papered over. The second
reliably produces a visibly better result — which teaches the real lesson:

**the first pass was never the ceiling.** Depth is a decision you make, not a
limit you accept.

## What a fabrication actually looks like

You saw one on Thursday, and you will see more. The thing to internalise:

**It does not look wrong.** A made-up citation has a plausible journal, a
plausible volume, a well-formed DOI, and the right authors doing the right kind
of work. It looks exactly like the real ones next to it. This is why "I'd notice"
is not a plan.

Three failures that are not the same failure — and telling them apart is most of
the skill:

| It says | It might mean |
|---|---|
| "I couldn't get the PDF" | the site blocks robots — the paper is free and fine |
| "It's paywalled" | it isn't; the download silently returned a login page |
| "I found it" | it resolved something *near* what you asked for |

> **"I couldn't get it" ≠ "it's paywalled" ≠ "it doesn't exist."**

And the one that catches everyone: **open the file it says it downloaded.** Some
publishers return a perfectly normal-looking "success" that is actually a login
page saved with a `.pdf` on the end.

## Where this stops being about the AI

Two of the traps you met this week were not the model's fault at all. A real
database had the wrong page numbers. A retracted paper's DOI still resolves and
still hands back a clean, normal-looking citation.

Nothing this week was invented to trip you up. Every trap was a real property of
a real literature.

**Which means the checking was always necessary — you just never had a tool fast
enough to make skipping it feel expensive.**

The agent doesn't make verification optional. It makes it cheap enough to
actually do.
