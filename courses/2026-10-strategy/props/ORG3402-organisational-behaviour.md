## Course description

Most of our graduates will spend their working lives inside organisations, and
most of what goes wrong in organisations is not technical. ORG 3402 introduces
the systematic study of how people actually behave at work: what motivates them,
how they succeed and fail in teams, how they are led, how they disagree, and how
they respond when the organisation around them changes.

The course follows three levels of analysis — the individual, the group, and the
organisation — in that order. We spend the first third on motivation, partly
because it underpins everything after it, and partly because it is the topic
students most often believe they already understand. The popular management
literature is full of confident claims that the research does not support.
Learning to tell the two apart is one of the main things this course is for.

Throughout, we treat organisational behaviour as an empirical field. Claims
arrive with evidence attached, and part of every session is asking how good that
evidence is. A theory that is famous is not thereby true, and a theory that is
old is not thereby wrong.

Students work in fixed teams of three from week 2 onwards — both because much of
the syllabus is about teams, and because the team is where students practise
giving and receiving structured peer feedback on written work.

## Learning outcomes

### Knowledge

On completion of the course, the student:

- **K1** — can explain the main content and process theories of work motivation,
  including Herzberg's two-factor theory, the job characteristics model,
  goal-setting theory and self-determination theory, and account for what each
  one claims that the others do not.
- **K2** — understands the importance of organisational culture for
  organisational performance.
- **K3** — can state what the empirical literature actually finds about the
  relationship between intra-group conflict and team performance, and identify
  where that finding departs from the standard textbook claim.

### Skills

On completion of the course, the student:

- **S1** — can apply a named motivation theory to a described workplace
  situation and derive at least two specific managerial actions from it, stating
  which element of the theory each action follows from.
- **S2** — can classify a conflict described in a case as primarily task-based
  or primarily relationship-based, and justify the classification with specific
  evidence quoted from the case.
- **S3** — is able to reflect critically on leadership in contemporary
  organisations.

### General competence

On completion of the course, the student:

- **G1** — can construct a written argument about an organisational problem in
  which every empirical claim is either supported by a cited source or
  explicitly marked as the student's own conjecture.
- **G2** — has developed an awareness of the ethical dimensions of managerial
  decisions and of the value of diversity in working life.

## Teaching and learning activities

Twelve sessions of two hours, one per week. Each session pairs a short lecture
with case discussion in fixed teams. Two of the sessions include a formative
in-class exercise that is marked by peers and not by the lecturer.

Attendance is not assessed. The reading, however, is not optional: the case
discussions assume it.

---

## Session plan

Chapter references are to the course textbook (see reading list).

**Session 1 — What is organisational behaviour?**
Levels of analysis: individual, group, organisation. Evidence-based management,
and why "common sense" is an unreliable guide to any of this.
*Core question: how would you know if a confident management claim were false?*
Reading: textbook ch. 1.

**Session 2 — Individual differences**
Personality and the Five-Factor Model, general mental ability, and how much of
workplace behaviour these actually explain. Selection as a prediction problem.
*Core question: how much of a person's performance is the person?*
Reading: textbook ch. 5.

**Session 3 — Motivation I: needs and content theories**
Maslow's hierarchy and its empirical status. Herzberg's two-factor theory:
hygiene factors and motivators, why Herzberg holds that satisfaction and
dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of one scale, and what follows for
managers who try to motivate people by improving conditions.
*Core question: you double someone's salary — what happens, and for how long?*
Reading: Herzberg (1968); textbook ch. 7.

**Session 4 — Motivation II: process theories**
Goal-setting theory: specificity, difficulty, commitment, feedback. Expectancy
theory and equity theory, and what each adds that the content theories miss.
*Core question: what makes a goal work, and when does a goal do damage?*
Reading: Locke & Latham (2002); textbook ch. 7.

**Session 5 — Motivation III: job design and self-determination**
The job characteristics model and its five core dimensions. Autonomy, competence
and relatedness. What the reward literature actually shows about paying people
for things they already enjoy.
*Core question: when does pay-for-performance backfire — and when does it not?*
Reading: Hackman & Oldham (1976); Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999); textbook ch. 8.

**Session 6 — Teams I: formation and development**
Roles, norms, status, size. Tuckman's stage model — what it says, and what kind
of evidence it was built from.
*Core question: is "storming" a finding or a story?*
Reading: Tuckman (1965); textbook ch. 9.

**Session 7 — Teams II: what makes teams work**
Process losses, social loafing, team composition, and psychological safety as a
condition for learning behaviour.
*Core question: why do teams of smart people underperform their own members?*
Reading: Edmondson (1999); textbook ch. 10.

**Session 8 — Conflict**
Task conflict versus relationship conflict. Jehn's original result, and the
meta-analysis that complicated it. Conflict management styles.
*Core question: is disagreement good for a team?*
Reading: Jehn (1995); De Dreu & Weingart (2003); textbook ch. 14.

**Session 9 — Leadership I: from traits to behaviour**
Trait approaches, the behavioural studies, contingency models — and why the
field kept moving on.
*Core question: are leaders born, made, or merely well matched to a situation?*
Reading: textbook ch. 12.

**Session 10 — Leadership II: transformational and transactional**
Bass's full-range model: the components of transformational leadership;
contingent reward and management-by-exception as the transactional side;
laissez-faire as the absence of both. The meta-analytic evidence on what
predicts what.
*Core question: which parts of "inspiring leadership" actually predict
performance?*
Reading: Judge & Piccolo (2004); textbook ch. 12.

**Session 11 — Organisational culture**
Schein's three levels: artifacts, espoused beliefs and values, and basic
underlying assumptions. Socialisation. National culture, and the trouble with
applying country-level averages to the person in front of you.
*Core question: can you change a culture on purpose?*
Reading: Schein & Schein (2017), ch. 1–3; textbook ch. 16.

**Session 12 — Organisational change**
Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze. Kotter's eight steps and the failure modes
they were derived from. What the quantitative literature says about how people
on the receiving end actually react.
*Core question: why do most change programmes fail, and is "resistance" the
right name for what happens?*
Reading: Kotter (1995); Oreg, Vakola & Armenakis (2011); textbook ch. 17.

## Assessment

| Part | Form | Weight | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Term paper, groups of 2–3, 2,500 words ±10% | 40% | Submitted in WISEflow, week 46 |
| 2 | Individual written examination, 3 hours, on campus | 60% | Exam period, December |

**Part 1 — Term paper.** Analyse an organisational problem of your choosing
using at least two theories from the course. The problem may be drawn from your
own work experience, from a published case, or from the news. Assesses learning
outcomes K1, K3, S1, S2 and G1.

**Part 2 — Written examination.** A mix of short-answer and essay questions.
Assesses all learning outcomes. Examination aids: none.

Both parts are graded on the A–F scale, and both must be passed to obtain a
grade in the course. Students who fail may retake the failed part at the next
ordinary examination.
